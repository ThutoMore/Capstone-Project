Heart Failure Mortality Prediction

Statistical, machine learning, and hybrid models compared end-to-end — from leakage detection to explainability — on 299 admission records.

01

Overview

299 heart failure patients, 11 admission-time predictors, no missing values, and a 32.1% mortality rate. Correlation analysis was the first step — and immediately surfaced the project's central risk.

What the correlations show

The follow-up-duration variable (time) correlates most strongly with the outcome (r = −0.53) — stronger than any clinical predictor. But it isn't known at admission, so it was excluded from every primary model and instead used for a dedicated leakage experiment (Section 3).

The next-strongest correlates are clinically well-established heart failure risk factors: serum_creatinine, ejection_fraction, and age — good face validity before any modeling began.

Reassuring signalFive independently-built models later converge on these same three predictors (Section 5).

04

Data Leakage: The time Variable

Both selected models were rebuilt and re-tuned from scratch with the follow-up-duration variable added, to isolate the leakage effect cleanly from stale hyperparameters.

Why this matters

Both models gain roughly +0.12 ROC-AUC when time is included. Since follow-up duration is only knowable in retrospect, this jump is leakage, not genuine predictive skill.

Excluding it — as done throughout this project — produces a model that scores lower on paper but is actually usable for a real patient at admission.

Strongest findingThis directly answers the research question: does excluding time improve generalizability? Yes — at a quantified, honest cost.
